Preface
Few groups of materials have changed a dental discipline as quickly as
bioceramics changed endodontics. In the span of a single generation,
mineral trioxide aggregate turned procedures that were once uncertain
— sealing a perforation, capping an exposed pulp, treating a tooth with
an open apex — into predictable, biologically driven treatments. The
materials that followed have widened that success and carried the same
chemistry into restorative dentistry, implantology and bone
regeneration.
The original monograph on which this edition is based set out the science
of these materials for the endodontic clinician with clarity and rigour.
This expanded edition keeps that foundation intact and adds three
things: illustration, so that mechanisms and clinical situations can be
seen as well as read; breadth, through two new chapters that place
endodontic bioceramics in the wider context of dentistry and of materials
science; and reference, through the full bibliography of the original work
and a set of quick-reference appendices.
The aim throughout has been to make the subject easier to learn without
diluting its accuracy. The illustrations are schematic, the comparative
figures are drawn from the cited literature, and the new background
chapters are written as established materials science rather than as
novel claims. Where the evidence is still incomplete — as it often is for
long-term clinical outcomes — the text says so plainly.
It is hoped that students, residents and practising clinicians alike will find
here both a readable introduction and a practical point of reference.

Bioceramics in medicine
Over the past several decades the materials available to medicine have
advanced dramatically, and ceramic materials in particular have been
engineered to repair and reconstruct the diseased or damaged parts of
the musculo-skeletal system. Ceramics used for this purpose are termed
bioceramics (Hench, 1991). Since the 1960s, alongside advances in
technology and knowledge, this family has grown to include alumina,
zirconia, coatings and composites, hydroxyapatite, bioactive glass,
glass-ceramics, resorbable calcium phosphates, radiotherapy glasses
and calcium silicate (Best et al., 2008; Dubok, 2000; Hench, 1991).
Hench (1991) and Best et al. (2008) classified bioceramics as bioinert,
bioactive and biodegradable (soluble or resorbable). In practice no
implant material is completely inert: every material provokes at least a
minimal reaction in living tissue (Dubok, 2000). A biomaterial, more
broadly, is any synthetic or natural material used to perform or
substitute a body function or tissue (Bandyopadhyay and Bose, 2013).
Figure 1.1 Bioceramics are grouped by how they interact with tissue; endodontic
materials are predominantly bioactive calcium-silicate cements and sealers.

Biomaterials can be described as biotoxic, bioinert, bioactive or
bioresorbable. Biotoxic materials — certain cadmium- or
vanadium-containing alloys, carbon steel and methyl methacrylate —
provoke pathological change or immune rejection. Bioinert materials
such as aluminium and zirconium oxides, tantalum and titanium coexist
with tissue, separated only by a thin fibrous layer. Bioactive materials —
high-density hydroxyapatite, tricalcium phosphate and certain bioglasses
— form direct biochemical bonds with tissue. Bioresorbable materials are
gradually resorbed and replaced by healthy tissue as the wound heals
(Dubok, 2000).
At a glance
• Bioceramics are the only synthetic inorganic materials that can be both
bioactive and bioresorbable.
• They provoke less immune response and offer a wider range of
mechanical and biochemical behaviour than most implants.
• Their main limitation is low crack resistance — they are brittle and hard
to adjust intra-operatively.
Because bioceramics are among the few inorganic materials that
demonstrate both bioactive and bioresorbable behaviour, and because
their biocompatibility exceeds that of most implanted materials, they
have been used extensively across surgical medicine (Dubok, 2000;
Hench, 1991). Their principal weakness relative to metals is low crack
resistance: they are brittle and difficult to manipulate. Combining
bioceramic particles with metallic implants captures the advantages of
both — carbon coatings, for example, are thromboresistant and are used
on prosthetic heart valves and orthopaedic and maxillofacial prostheses
(Geesink and Manley, 1993; Hench, 1991).
Bioinert ceramics retain their characteristics indefinitely within the body,
which makes them valuable for immunocompromised and oncological
patients. Aluminium oxide (corundum) was the first widely used
bioceramic thanks to its high wear resistance, strength and corrosion
resistance; it is used in load-bearing hip prostheses, dental implants,
knee prostheses, alveolar-ridge and maxillofacial reconstruction, bone
screws, and even replacements for the cornea and the ossicles of the

middle ear (Hench, 1991; Hulbert et al., 1987). Despite its compatibility,
alumina has low crack resistance; zirconium dioxide offers similar
biocompatibility with greater bend strength and crack resistance,
especially when stabilised with yttrium or cerium oxide (Dubok, 2000).
Bioactive and bioresorbable ceramics are used to aid healing of bone
defects after removal of cysts and tumours: bioactive ceramics integrate
with bone and hold their strength during healing, while bioresorbable
ceramics are gradually replaced by new bone. Between them they serve
maxillofacial reconstruction, cranial repair, bone grafting, joint
reconstruction, hearing endoprostheses and cosmetic eye prostheses;
bioresorbable ceramics can also support restoration of tendons,
ligaments, nerve fibres and blood vessels (Black, 1988; Hulbert et al.,
1987). The surface biochemical bonding that defines bioactivity was first
detected in the late 1960s (Hench et al., 1971).
Bioceramics in dentistry
Bioceramics are not new to dentistry. Hulbert et al. (1987) reviewed
calcium-phosphate bioceramics used more than two decades earlier for
periodontal treatment, alveolar-ridge augmentation, maxillofacial
surgery and dental implants. Hench (1991) noted the use of alumina,
hydroxyapatite and bioactive glasses for dental implants and
maxillofacial reconstruction, and of hydroxyapatite, tricalcium
phosphate, calcium-phosphate salts and bioactive glasses for
periodontal-pocket obliteration. Their excellent physicochemical and
biological properties made these materials natural candidates for
endodontics.
Bioceramics in endodontics
Bioceramics entered endodontics in the 1990s in the form of mineral
trioxide aggregate (MTA), first used as a retrograde (root-end) filling and
later for root repair and as a coating for gutta-percha cones (Koch and
Brave, 2012). Advances in nanotechnology have since widened their use
to root-canal sealing. The reasons for choosing them are compelling:
bioceramics are highly biocompatible, non-toxic, chemically stable in a
biological environment and do not shrink on setting. An overfill during
obturation or root repair does not provoke an inflammatory reaction, and

the materials form hydroxyapatite that strengthens the bond to dentine

Figure 1.2 Bioceramics migrated from general medicine to dentistry and, from 1993,

Bioceramics are notably forgiving in technique because blood and
moisture do not contaminate them; indeed they require moisture to set
(Hench, 1991; Jefferies, 2014). They offer excellent sealing because they
remain dimensionally stable and expand marginally on setting (Gandolfi
et al., 2009; Jefferies, 2014). On setting, the hydration reaction liberates
calcium hydroxide, which dissociates into calcium and hydroxyl ions and
raises the pH above 12 (Jefferies, 2014; Richardson, 2008). While unset
they are antibacterial; once set they become biocompatible and
bioactive, forming hydroxyapatite in tissue fluids as released calcium
hydroxide reacts with phosphates (Richardson, 2008). This apatite
formation underlies their tissue-inductive behaviour.
Why bioceramics changed endodontics
• Not moisture-sensitive — they set in the wet field of the root canal.
• High pH on setting → antibacterial while unset; bioactive once set.
• Dimensionally stable with marginal expansion → excellent seal, no
shrinkage.
• Form hydroxyapatite and bond to dentine → tissue-inductive healing.
The technology has changed how endodontists plan treatment, surgically
and non-surgically, improving the prognosis of teeth that would once
have been lost. Bioceramics are now a first choice for pulpotomy, pulp

2.1 Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA)
MTA powder is essentially a purified Portland cement: tricalcium silicate,
dicalcium silicate, tricalcium aluminate, calcium sulfate dihydrate
(gypsum), calcium aluminoferrite and bismuth oxide. It is mixed with
sterile water at roughly a 3:1 powder-to-water ratio (Torabinejad et al.,
1993). Two reactions drive setting. In the first, the silicate phases
hydrate to form calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) and calcium hydroxide;
the calcium hydroxide raises pH, confers antibacterial activity and seeds
the deposition of mineralised apatite. In the second, tricalcium aluminate
reacts with water in the presence of calcium sulfate to form ettringite,
and — once the sulfate phases are consumed — monosulfate (Camilleri
et al., 2013).
Figure 2.1 The silicate (first) reaction generates the high pH and apatite-forming
behaviour that make MTA bioactive; the aluminate (second) reaction governs early set.
Key point — moisture is a friend, not an enemy
• Most dental materials degrade in a wet field. MTA is the opposite: it
requires moisture to set, which is why it was introduced to overcome
contamination problems (Shah et al., 1996).

MTA is sold in grey (GMTA) and white (WMTA) forms; WMTA was
developed to reduce the dentine discolouration seen with the original
grey material (Kratchman, 2004). Its chief drawback is a long setting
time — a mean of about 165 minutes for conventional MTA (Gandolfi et
al., 2009; Torabinejad et al., 1995a) — although WMTA sets faster than
GMTA (Islam et al., 2006). A long set makes it hard to maintain a
consistent mix, whereas a short set favours a tight seal between canal
and periodontium (Santos et al., 2008).
Figure 2.2 Conventional MTA sets slowly; later products such as MTA Angelus and
Biodentine were engineered for a much shorter set.
Any material that permanently seals a canal should be alkaline and
should release calcium ions to promote mineralised-apatite deposition —
both in-vitro and in-vivo work links pulp wound healing to an alkaline pH
and calcium-ion availability (Holland et al., 2001; Okabe et al., 2006).
The pH of MTA rises from about 10.2 immediately after mixing to 12.5 by
three hours (Torabinejad et al., 1995a); GMTA shows a lower value at 60
minutes than WMTA (Chng et al., 2005). The high pH reflects continuous
liberation of calcium ions and formation of calcium hydroxide (Fridland
and Rosado, 2005; Han and Okiji, 2013).

radiographically (Torabinejad et al., 1995a). Bismuth oxide is added to

Solubility should be low for root-end and repair materials; MTA showed
little or no solubility in most studies, though one long-term study
reported an increase (Fridland and Rosado, 2005; Poggio et al., 2007;
Shie et al., 2009). Porosity should also be low, since mechanical strength
rises as porosity falls (Holt et al., 2007); an acidic environment and air
bubbles introduced during mixing both raise porosity (Namazikhah et al.,
2008), while chelating agents, low humidity, high condensation forces
and low pH can reduce microhardness (Nekoofar et al., 2007).
Compressive strength depends on the type of MTA and is higher for
conventional MTA than for MTA Angelus (Basturk et al., 2013);
phosphoric-acid etching lowers it (Kayahan et al., 2009). Amalgam, IRM
and Super-EBA initially exceed MTA in compressive strength, but the
difference disappears by three weeks (Torabinejad et al., 1995a). Placing
a wet cotton pellet over MTA for a day can raise its flexural strength by
exposing it to moisture on two sides (Walker et al., 2006). Push-out bond
strength is pH-sensitive: it is highest near pH 8.4 and falls in more acidic
conditions (Saghiri et al., 2010; Shokouhinejad et al., 2010).
Biomineralisation arises from the slow release of calcium ions during
setting, which up-regulates signalling molecules — transforming growth
factor-β, macrophage colony-stimulating factor and interleukins — that
recruit progenitor cells and drive their differentiation into

odontoblast-like cells and dentinogenesis (Koh et al., 1997; Okiji and
Yoshiba, 2009). MTA can induce an osteogenic phenotype, up-regulating
osteonectin, osteopontin and alkaline phosphatase (Bonson et al., 2004),
and the resulting mineral tags within dentinal tubules improve push-out

Biocompatibility is well documented: MTA outperformed amalgam, IRM,
Super-EBA, gutta-percha and several glass ionomers in cytotoxicity and
cell-attachment studies (Osorio et al., 1998; Souza et al., 2006;
Yoshimine et al., 2007) and is comparable to the newer bioceramics —
BC Sealer, ERRM and Biodentine (Ma et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2010b;
Zhou et al., 2013). Reports on its antibacterial effect conflict, but
consensus holds that its high pH and calcium-hydroxide formation give it
real antimicrobial activity, with differences between studies explained by
the type and amount of MTA and the organisms tested (Parirokh and
Torabinejad, 2010a).
2.2 MTA Angelus
Developed in Brazil, MTA Angelus is 80% Portland cement and 20%
bismuth oxide, with the calcium sulfate removed to shorten the set
(Duarte et al., 2003). Its setting time of about 14 minutes is far shorter
than WMTA or GMTA (Chng et al., 2005; Santos et al., 2008). Its finer,
more variable particle size (48 μm vs 65 μm for MTA) improves handling
(Komabayashi and Spångberg, 2008). Reports on pH and calcium-ion
release conflict — some find lower values than MTA, some higher, some
no difference — probably because measurements were taken at different
times after mixing (Cavenago et al., 2014; Duarte et al., 2003;
Reyes-Carmona et al., 2009). Its radiopacity is lower than WMTA and
GMTA (Chng et al., 2005).
MTA Angelus is biocompatible: neither grey nor white forms were
genotoxic or cytotoxic to Chinese-hamster ovary or lymphoma cells at
1–1000 μg/mL (Ribeiro et al., 2006a, 2006b). It stimulated mineralisation
in rat connective tissue (Gomes-Filho et al., 2009) and — with GMTA and
Portland cement — was effective against the organisms tested, including
E. coli (Tanomaru-Filho et al., 2007).
2.3 Biodentine

Biodentine combines tricalcium and dicalcium silicate with calcium
carbonate and a water-based liquid containing calcium chloride as a
setting accelerator. It sets in about 10–12 minutes and exceeds
3 mm-aluminium radiopacity (Grech et al., 2013b), which suits it to
perforation repair. It is marketed as a dentine substitute for perforation
repair and coronal restoration and can be used for pulp capping (File,
2010). Rheometry shows it has the shortest set among tricalcium-silicate
cements (Setbon et al., 2014), and it produces only slight clinical

Immersed in Hank's balanced solution, Biodentine held a pH of about

MTA, BC Sealer, BioAggregate, IRM and TCS-Zr (Grech et al., 2013a; Han

strength are high (Guneser et al., 2013). A small particle size (~5 μm)
and homogeneous composition let it diffuse into dentinal tubules and
form mineral-infiltration zones and tag-like structures, giving greater
push-out strength than MTA and a cohesive rather than adhesive failure
mode (Atmeh et al., 2012; Guneser et al., 2013).

ionomer, though some cultured cell lines showed lower viability with
Biodentine than MTA at day seven (Pérard et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2013)
and both lowered proliferation of OD-21 pulp cells relative to control (Luo
et al., 2014). Such discrepancies underline the need for well-designed,
randomised long-term clinical trials; in-vitro and in-vivo evidence is
generally favourable but long-term clinical data remain limited.

minimal chemical change under vertical gutta-percha compaction

A systematic review of pre-mixed calcium-silicate sealers found good
in-vitro physicochemical and biological properties and results in animal
studies that matched or exceeded conventional sealers, while noting the
shortage of well-designed long-term clinical trials (Almeida et al., 2017).
Trope et al. (2015) summarised the class: highly hydrophilic, so canal
moisture helps rather than harms; unset pH above 12 for antibacterial
activity; setting at variable rates in different environments; slight
expansion without shrinkage; insolubility in tissue fluids; and bonding to
bioceramic-coated gutta-percha. Randomised double-blind trials remain
necessary to confirm clinical performance.

1. Retrograde filling
Endodontic surgery preserves teeth with post-treatment periapical
disease when orthograde retreatment cannot address the cause — apical

apical surgery has become microsurgery, adding magnification,
illumination, microinstruments, ultrasonic contra-angle tips and more
biocompatible materials (Kim and Kratchman, 2006). After the root end
is resected, a retrograde filling seals the canal from the apical direction.

2. Perforation repair
2.1 Furcal perforations
Furcation perforations connect the root-canal space to the periradicular
tissues, arising from iatrogenic error or from pathology such as caries
and resorption (AAE, 2015). The resulting inflammation damages the
periodontal ligament and resorbs bone and dental tissue, and can rapidly
open a route to the gingival sulcus with irreversible loss of attachment.

as Biodentine — without staining heavy metals, with good sealing,
antimicrobial activity and a shorter set — were developed as alternatives
(Kang et al., 2015). Biodentine allowed formation of mineralised bridges
thicker than those with MTA after pulpotomy in dogs (De Rossi et al.,

2.2 Lateral root perforations
Lateral perforations can occur at any canal level; the more coronal the
site, the easier it is to repair the perforation and obturate the canal
separately. The canal is usually filled first, carefully avoiding sealer
contamination of the perforation, and during repair the cement is kept
from dropping into the empty canal. Success depends on the elapsed
time before treatment and the perforation's location and size (Fuss and
Trope, 1996). Ford et al. (1995) found no clear link between size and
prognosis, and MTA has healed large perforations in case reports (Arens
and Torabinejad, 1996).
Location is decisive. Fuss and Trope (1996) defined the critical zone at
the crestal bone and epithelial attachment: perforations apical or coronal
to it have a good prognosis, whereas crestal perforations invite epithelial
migration and periodontal breakdown and heal least well; a periodontal
defect connecting the mouth to the perforation worsens the outlook
(Ford et al., 1995). Treatment should be immediate sealing with a
biocompatible material that is insoluble in tissue fluids and promotes
tissue regeneration (Beavers et al., 1986; Holland et al., 2007). Older
materials — amalgam, Cavit, calcium hydroxide, Super-EBA,
calcium-phosphate cement, glass ionomer, decalcified freeze-dried bone
— are insufficiently biocompatible against bone, so teeth with
perforations carried an uncertain prognosis before MTA (Farzaneh et al.,
2004; Fuss and Trope, 1996). MTA has since proved successful for both
furcal and lateral perforations (Main et al., 2004; Regan et al., 2002).

3. Internal inflammatory root resorption
3.1 Internal resorption in the root canal
Internal resorption follows destruction of the organic sheath, predentine
and odontoblast layer, exposing mineralised dentine to pulpal cells with
resorptive potential. Contrary to earlier belief it is often associated with
pulpitis and stops once the pulp becomes necrotic (Gabor et al., 2012).
Predisposing factors include trauma, pulpotomy, transplantation,
invagination, restorative and orthodontic procedures, and even
herpes-zoster infection. It is rarer than external resorption and can be
confused with external cervical resorption, risking misdiagnosis (Frank,
1981; Patel et al., 2009).
Once confirmed and if the tooth is restorable, conventional root-canal
treatment is performed to remove vital and necrotic remnants that could
feed the resorbing cells and to disinfect and fill the system
(Endodontology, 2006). Where the root is not perforated,
chemo-mechanical debridement with calcium hydroxide (for large
defects) followed by gutta-percha and sealer is sufficient — bioceramics
are not specifically required (Haapasalo et al., 2015). Once resorption
has perforated the root, however, bioceramic cements are an excellent
choice: they tolerate moisture and small amounts of blood, seal well to
dentine, are antimicrobial and have dentine-like mechanical properties
(Altundasar and Demir, 2009; Torabinejad et al., 1995a).
3.2 Internal resorption in the pulp chamber
Non-perforating chamber resorption does not require bioceramics and is
straightforward to manage. If a perforation has occurred, the material is
chosen by size and location: bioceramic cements are tapped gently

4. Invasive cervical resorption
4.1 Type I and II cervical resorption
Invasive (external) cervical resorption (ECR) begins just below the
epithelial attachment and remains poorly understood. Defects in the
cementum layer expose dentine to clastic cells, and the anatomy of the
cemento-enamel junction — where cemental gaps are common — leaves
dentine unprotected and liable to osteoclastic resorption (Fuss et al.,
2003; Neuvald and Consolaro, 2000). Predisposing factors include
trauma, orthodontics, internal bleaching, surgery, periodontal therapy,
bruxism, intracoronal restorations, developmental defects and systemic
disease (Heithersay, 1999; Patel et al., 2009). ECR is usually aseptic but
can be secondarily colonised by micro-organisms from the sulcus or a
necrotic pulp (Heithersay, 2004; Trope, 2002).
Heithersay (2004) classified ECR by depth of invasion: Class 1, shallow
and cervical; Class 2, into coronal dentine near the pulp; Class 3, into the
coronal third of the root; Class 4, beyond the coronal third. Clinically it
presents in the cervical region, sometimes as a pink spot, with a positive
vitality response until the pulp is involved; advanced lesions bleed
profusely on probing and have sharp cavity margins (Patel et al., 2009).

6. Pulp capping and vital pulp therapy
Preserving pulp vitality is vital in immature permanent teeth, where
continued vitality allows complete root formation (Laurent et al., 2012).
Vital pulp therapy aims to retain the pulp and induce the pulp–dentine
complex to regenerate and form tertiary dentine — especially important
where the apex is still developing (Bogen and Chandler, 2008). Options
range from indirect and direct pulp capping to partial and full pulpotomy,
chosen by the extent of pulp involvement.

The American Association of Endodontists defines direct pulp capping
(DPC) as sealing an exposed vital pulp with a material such as calcium
hydroxide or MTA to promote reparative dentine and maintain vitality
(AAE, 2003). The ideal capping material seals the wound, differentiates
pulp cells into odontoblasts, is biocompatible and antimicrobial, and has
adequate mechanical strength (Koh et al., 1997; Zhang et al., 2013).
Calcium hydroxide was traditional but has drawbacks — tunnel defects in
the dentine bridge, high solubility, chamber obliteration, poor adhesion
and degradation after acid etching (Asgary and Ahmadyar, 2013; Stanley
and Pameijer, 1997) — so MTA and Biodentine are now preferred.
MTA induces dentinogenesis more quickly and homogeneously than
calcium hydroxide and encourages granulation-tissue formation that aids
healing (Dammaschke et al., 2010); bioceramics also surpass calcium
hydroxide in mechanical properties, sealing and often biocompatibility
(Ma et al., 2011). A randomised trial of 376 patients confirmed MTA's
superiority as a DPC material at two years (Hilton et al., 2013), and in
carious molar exposures MTA outperformed calcium hydroxide (85% vs
52% success; Kundzina et al., 2016). MTA and Biodentine each achieved

leaving thin, fracture-prone walls — the immature tooth. Apexification

Calcium hydroxide apexification is effective (success up to ~90%) but

The endodontic materials described so far are one branch of a much
larger family. The same ceramic chemistry — and the same idea of a
material that is either inert, bioactive or resorbable — runs through
almost every discipline of dentistry, from the crown that restores a tooth
to the graft that rebuilds the bone beneath it. This chapter surveys that
wider landscape and shows how the properties emphasised earlier
(biocompatibility, apatite formation, alkalinity, strength) reappear in new
clinical roles.

5.1 A common chemistry, many roles
It is useful to separate two broad groups. Structural (restorative)
ceramics — dental porcelains and oxide ceramics — are prized chiefly for

Bioactive and resorbable bioceramics — calcium silicates,

ions, they template new mineral and, in some cases, they are replaced
by the tissue they help to heal. Endodontic bioceramics belong firmly to
the second group, but a complete picture of dentistry requires both.
5.2 Restorative dentistry: liners, bases and
dentine substitutes
Beneath a filling, the pulp must be protected from thermal, chemical and
bacterial insult. For decades calcium hydroxide was the standard liner
over deep dentine and near exposures, prized for its alkalinity and its
ability to stimulate reparative dentine — but limited by solubility, low
strength and a porous dentine bridge. Calcium-silicate materials have
changed this picture. The same tricalcium-silicate chemistry that makes
MTA valuable in the root canal makes it, and its faster-setting relatives,
excellent dentine substitutes.
Biodentine is marketed explicitly as a dentine-replacement material: it
can line or base a deep cavity, cap an exposed pulp, and even serve as a
temporary full-dentine restoration under a definitive coronal restoration,
thanks to its short set, useful strength and calcium release. Light-cured
calcium-silicate liners add resin to allow on-command setting and easy
placement as a thin protective layer under composite. In each case the
attraction is bioactivity — an alkaline, calcium-releasing surface that
encourages mineralisation — combined with better mechanical
behaviour and lower solubility than calcium hydroxide. Conventional and
resin-modified glass-ionomer cements, while not calcium-silicate
ceramics, occupy an adjacent niche: they bond to tooth structure,
release fluoride and are often described as bioactive, and they remain
widely used as liners, bases and restorations.

The clinical behaviour of a bioceramic follows directly from a handful of
measurable properties. Understanding how each is defined and tested
makes the comparisons in earlier chapters easier to interpret and helps
the clinician read a manufacturer’s data sheet critically.
6.1 Setting chemistry
Calcium-silicate bioceramics set by hydration, not by drying: water
reacts with the silicate phases to form calcium-silicate-hydrate gel and
calcium hydroxide (see Figure 2.1). The calcium hydroxide dissociates to
raise pH and supply calcium ions, while the hydrate gel provides
structure. Because water drives the reaction, these materials tolerate —
indeed require — the moist environment that degrades many
conventional cements, a property that recurs in every clinical application
from root-end filling to implant coatings.
6.2 How key properties are measured
Radiopacity lets the clinician confirm a filling radiographically. It is
measured against an aluminium step-wedge and expressed as an
equivalent thickness of aluminium; international standards set a
minimum of about 3 mm. Radiopacifiers such as bismuth oxide or
zirconium oxide are added to reach clinically useful values (compare
Figure 2.4).
Setting time is determined with standardised indenter (Gilmore or Vicat)
needles, recording the initial set (when the surface first resists
indentation) and the final set. For calcium-silicate materials the result
depends strongly on water availability, particle size and any
accelerators, which is why products range from a few minutes to several
hours (Figure 2.2).

Solubility and dimensional stability matter because a material that
dissolves or shrinks loses its seal. Solubility is assessed by mass loss
after immersion; a good root-end or repair material shows very low
solubility and slight expansion rather than shrinkage. Flow, important for
sealers, is measured by compressing a fixed volume between plates and
recording the disc diameter, against a standardised minimum.
Mechanical properties are characterised by compressive and flexural
strength, microhardness, and — specific to endodontics — push-out bond
strength, which measures resistance to displacement from the canal
wall. These values are sensitive to mixing, porosity, environmental pH
and time, so laboratory figures should be read as comparative rather
than absolute.
pH and calcium-ion release are followed over time in solution. The
characteristic rise to a strongly alkaline pH (Figure 2.3) reflects
continuing calcium-hydroxide formation and is responsible both for
antibacterial activity while the material is unset and for the apatite
formation that follows.
6.3 Biological evaluation
Biocompatibility is not a single measurement but a hierarchy of
evidence. Cytotoxicity assays (for example the MTT and XTT tests)
expose cultured cells to the material or its extracts and measure
survival; cell-attachment and morphology studies assess how well cells
settle and spread on the set surface. Biomineralisation markers —
alkaline phosphatase, osteopontin, osteonectin and transcription factors
such as RUNX2 — indicate whether a material is actively driving cells
toward a hard-tissue-forming phenotype. Above these sit animal studies
and, at the top, randomised controlled clinical trials. As the endodontic
chapters repeatedly note, the laboratory and animal evidence for
bioceramics is strong, while long-term randomised clinical data remain
the principal gap.

6.4 Handling and clinical tips
Several practical points recur across applications. Mix to the
manufacturer’s ratio and avoid incorporating air, which raises porosity
and lowers strength. Provide adequate — but not excessive — moisture:
too little prevents set, too much can prolong it and soften the surface.
Place the material without voids and, where a slow-setting cement is
used, protect it (for example with a moist cotton pellet) until set is
confirmed. Where discolouration matters, prefer white or
heavy-metal-free formulations. Finally, a bioceramic seal is only as
durable as the coronal restoration above it: a sound, well-sealed
restoration is essential to prevent leakage and protect the result.

6.5 Limitations and precautions
For all their advantages, bioceramics have real constraints. Like all
ceramics they are brittle, with limited crack resistance. Some
formulations discolour tooth or soft tissue through bismuth or iron oxide.
Set material can be difficult to remove if retreatment is later needed.
Slow-setting products demand patience or protective measures, and cost
can exceed that of traditional materials. Above all, the clinical evidence
base, though growing, still lacks the long-term randomised trials that
would place many indications beyond doubt. These limitations do not
diminish the value of the materials, but they define where care and
judgement are required.

About this chapter
This chapter draws on the adhesion literature — in particular the account of
enamel and dentine bonding by Breschi, Ferracane, Cadenaro, Mazzoni and

Almost every modern restoration is held in place, at least in part, by
adhesion. Where earlier dentistry relied on mechanical retention —
undercuts, pins, cements that merely filled a gap — adhesive dentistry
bonds the restoration to the tooth, allowing more conservative
preparations, better sealing and tooth-coloured materials. For the
endodontically treated tooth in particular, a reliable adhesive seal over
the access cavity is what protects the bioceramic root filling beneath it.
This chapter explains how dental adhesives work and where they
intersect with bioceramic materials.
7.1 Why adhesion matters
Adhesion serves three linked goals: retention of the restoration, a
marginal seal against bacterial leakage, and reinforcement of the
remaining tooth structure. A tooth restored with a well-bonded material
behaves more like an intact tooth and is less prone to leakage and
secondary caries. Because leakage is the common pathway to failure of
both restorations and root-canal treatments, the quality of the adhesive
seal is not a cosmetic detail but a determinant of long-term success.
7.2 The two substrates: enamel and dentine
Enamel and dentine could hardly be more different, and bonding to each
is a distinct problem. Enamel is highly mineralised, relatively dry and
uniform; etching it with phosphoric acid — the technique first
demonstrated by Buonocore in 1955 (Buonocore, 1955) — dissolves a
few micrometres of mineral to create a microporous, high-energy surface
into which fluid resin flows and, once cured, locks as countless
microscopic tags. Enamel bonding is therefore strong, durable and
predictable.

-- 62 of 132 --

Bioceramic in Dentistry 63
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
Dentine is the harder problem. It is less mineralised, intrinsically wet
(threaded by fluid-filled tubules connected to the pulp), and rich in
collagen. Cutting dentine also leaves a smear layer — a micrometre-thick
film of cut debris that plugs the tubule mouths. A dentine adhesive must
manage this moisture, deal with the smear layer, and bond to a surface
that is part mineral and part organic. The solution is not glue but
micromechanical infiltration, embodied in the hybrid layer.
7.3 The hybrid layer
When dentine is conditioned, the surface mineral is removed to expose a
scaffold of collagen fibrils; a fluid resin then infiltrates this scaffold and is
polymerised in place. The resulting hybrid layer — resin entangled within
demineralised collagen, continuous with resin tags that extend into the
tubules — is the true basis of dentine bonding. First described by
Nakabayashi and co-workers in 1982 (Nakabayashi et al., 1982), it
explains why technique matters so much: the resin must infiltrate to the
full depth that the acid demineralised, or an unprotected, weak zone of
bare collagen is left behind.

7.4 Etching strategies and adhesive families

deliver the resin. Two philosophies dominate. Etch-and-rinse (total-etch)
systems apply phosphoric acid first, which is then rinsed away together
with the smear layer, before priming and bonding; they come in
three-step (etch, prime, bond) and two-step (etch, then combined
prime-and-bond) forms. Self-etch systems instead use acidic monomers
that condition and prime simultaneously and incorporate the smear layer
rather than removing it; they come in two-step and one-step (all-in-one)
forms.

forms a stable chemical bond to calcium in hydroxyapatite and also

address. In general, etch-and-rinse gives the strongest enamel bond,
while self-etch is less technique-sensitive on dentine and causes less
post-operative sensitivity; selective enamel etching with a universal
adhesive seeks the best of both.

7.6 Durability: why bonds age
The immediate bond strength of modern adhesives is high; the challenge
is keeping it. Over months and years the hybrid layer is attacked on two
fronts. Hydrolysis — water slowly breaking down resin and any exposed
collagen — degrades the interface, a process visible as nanoleakage
(tracer penetration into nanometre-scale gaps; Sano et al., 1995). At the
same time, the tooth’s own enzymes, the matrix metalloproteinases and
cysteine cathepsins, are activated by acidic conditioning and digest
unprotected collagen within the hybrid layer (Pashley et al., 2004).

that form more hydrolytically stable chemical bonds; enzyme inhibitors

surface treatment of the restoration, give the strongest and best-sealing
results and are essential for materials that rely on bonding for strength,
such as thin veneers and glass-ceramics. They are broadly grouped as

strength, technique sensitivity and substrate: a bonded lithium-disilicate
veneer demands a full adhesive protocol with silane, whereas a highly
retentive zirconia crown may be luted more simply with an
MDP-containing cement. Moisture control during luting is as decisive
here as in direct restoration.
7.9 Bonding to root dentine: posts and cores
The endodontically treated tooth often lacks enough coronal structure to
retain a restoration, and a post may be placed in the canal to retain a
core that in turn supports a crown. Modern practice favours fibre posts
bonded adhesively into the root, because their elasticity is closer to that
of dentine than a rigid metal post and they distribute stress more
favourably. Bonding into a root canal is, however, one of the most
demanding adhesive situations: access is deep and narrow, moisture
control is difficult, curing light barely reaches the apical portion, and
residues of endodontic sealers — especially eugenol-containing ones —
can impair the bond. This is a further reason resin-compatible bioceramic
sealers are attractive when an adhesive post is anticipated. The unifying
principle remains that the adhesive restoration and the bioceramic root
filling protect the tooth only if the whole coronal seal holds.
7.10 Isolation, moisture control and common
failures

-- 68 of 132 --

Bioceramic in Dentistry 69
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
More adhesive failures come from technique than from the choice of
product. The single most important factor is isolation: a dry, saliva-free
and blood-free field — most reliably a rubber dam — is fundamental,
because contamination of an etched or primed surface ruins the bond.
Beyond isolation, the common pitfalls are recognisable and avoidable:
1. Contamination of the bonding surface with saliva, blood or handpiece
oil after conditioning.
2. Over-drying etched dentine (collapsing the collagen mesh), or leaving
it visibly wet.
3. Incomplete evaporation of the adhesive solvent before curing.
4. Inadequate light-curing — too brief, too far away, or with a weak
curing light.
5. Eugenol or other resin-inhibiting residues left on the bonding surface.
6. Bonding to an immature calcium-silicate surface before it has set and
matured.
Because each of these is under the clinician’s control, meticulous
technique — not merely material selection — is what turns a good
adhesive into a durable, well-sealed restoration.
7.11 Bioactive and self-adhesive restoratives: a
convergence
The sharp line once drawn between ‘adhesive’ and ‘bioactive’ materials
is blurring. Glass-ionomer and resin-modified glass-ionomer cements
already combine adhesion to tooth structure with fluoride release and
are often described as bioactive; newer ion-releasing composites,
giomers and calcium- or phosphate-releasing restoratives extend the
idea, aiming to seal a cavity adhesively while also buffering acid,
releasing mineral-forming ions and encouraging remineralisation at the
margin.
For the clinician this convergence is practical rather than abstract. A
single material that bonds well and behaves bioactively could simplify
the layered approach — bioceramic base, adhesive, composite — that is
common today, and could improve the durability of the margin, the

commonest site of restoration failure. The evidence base for these newer
materials is still maturing, and their bond strengths and long-term
behaviour vary; but the direction of travel is clear, and it brings the two
subjects of this book steadily closer together.

CHAPTER EIGHT
Reaction of Periodontal
Tissues to Materials
How the periodontium responds — and why it favours bioceramics

-- 71 of 132 --

Bioceramic in Dentistry 72
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
About this chapter
This chapter draws together the tissue-response themes of the endodontic
chapters. Its discussion of endodontic-sealer biocompatibility is supported by
author–year citations to the primary literature reviewed by Dassieu (2023)
and listed under ‘Additional references’; the broader histological trends are
discussed, with their sources, in Chapters Two to Four.
Many of the procedures in this book place a material in direct, lasting
contact with the periodontium — the ligament, cementum, bone and
gingiva that surround and support the tooth. A root-end filling faces the
apical periodontium; a perforation repair faces the lateral or furcal
periodontium; a resorption repair or a subgingival margin faces the
cervical periodontium and gingiva. How these tissues react to the
material is therefore central to the outcome, and it is one of the
strongest arguments for bioceramics.
8.1 The periodontium and where materials meet
it
The periodontium has four components: the gingiva at the margin, the
periodontal ligament (PDL) suspending the root, the cementum covering
the root surface, and the alveolar bone forming the socket. Dental
materials contact these tissues wherever the root surface or the pulp
space is breached — at a resected apex, a perforation, a resorptive
cavity, or a restoration margin that lies at or below the gingival crest.

-- 72 of 132 --

materials come into contact with them.
8.2 The spectrum of tissue response
A material in contact with periodontal tissue can provoke responses
along a spectrum. At best it is biocompatible and even bioactive —
tolerated without inflammation and actively encouraging repair, with
new hard tissue forming on its surface. In the middle it is merely
tolerated, separated from the tissue by a thin fibrous capsule. At worst it
is irritant or cytotoxic, sustaining chronic inflammation, resorption and
failure of healing. The clinical goal is to sit as far toward the bioactive
end of this spectrum as possible, especially where the material will
remain in contact with the tissue permanently.
8.3 How traditional materials behave
The materials that preceded bioceramics generally sit in the middle or
unfavourable part of the spectrum. Amalgam, long used for root-end
fillings, is tolerated but does not encourage regeneration and can cause
discolouration and corrosion products in the tissue. Zinc-oxide-eugenol
materials (including IRM) release eugenol, which is irritant and cytotoxic

-- 73 of 132 --

Bioceramic in Dentistry 74
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
in the concentrations found at a fresh interface. Super-EBA and
reinforced cements improved handling and sealing but remain
essentially non-regenerative. Glass-ionomer cements are better
tolerated and release fluoride, but do not reliably induce new cementum
or bone against the periodontium. Against this background the arrival of
materials that actively favour periodontal healing was a significant
advance.
8.4 The favourable response to bioceramics
Calcium-silicate bioceramics elicit a notably favourable periodontal
response. Histological studies of materials such as MTA repeatedly show
three encouraging features: the deposition of new cementum directly on
the material surface (cementogenesis), the re-establishment of an
organised periodontal ligament, and healing of the adjacent alveolar
bone — often with a hard-tissue barrier forming across the interface and
minimal inflammatory infiltrate. In effect the material is not merely
tolerated but is incorporated into the repairing periodontium.
Figure 8.2 Over a bioceramic root-end filling, tissues tend to heal constructively: new
cementum bridges the surface, ligament fibres reattach and bone fills in.

-- 74 of 132 --

Expanded & Illustrated Edition
This behaviour is what allows bioceramics to change the prognosis of
teeth that would once have been lost — perforated teeth, teeth needing
apical surgery, teeth with resorptive defects communicating with the
periodontium. The material does not simply plug a defect; it provides a
surface on which the periodontium can rebuild.
8.5 Why: cellular and molecular mechanisms
The favourable response follows directly from the chemistry emphasised
throughout this book. As the material sets and matures it maintains a
high pH, releases calcium ions, and forms a surface apatite layer. This
microenvironment supports the attachment and differentiation of the key
periodontal cells: cementoblasts that lay down cementum, PDL
fibroblasts that organise ligament fibres, and osteoblasts that form bone.
The released calcium and the alkaline conditions up-regulate
mineralisation signalling — including transforming growth factor-β and
bone morphogenetic proteins — and markers of hard-tissue formation
such as alkaline phosphatase and osteopontin.

8.6 Clinical settings

The same favourable interaction recurs across the clinical situations
described in Chapter Four. In perforation repair, a bioceramic seal
against the furcal or lateral periodontium limits inflammation and
supports bone and cementum repair, where older materials invited
attachment loss. In root-end filling, it promotes periradicular
regeneration with new cementum and ligament over the resected
surface. In invasive cervical resorption and other defects that
communicate with the periodontium, its tolerance of moisture and its
regenerative surface make it well suited to restoring the lost tissue. In
regenerative endodontics, the coronal bioceramic plug sits against
tissues from which new vital tissue is expected to grow.
8.7 A comparative view
Bringing these observations together, the materials used against the
periodontium can be ranked qualitatively by the tissue response they
tend to provoke. Calcium-silicate bioceramics and Biodentine sit at the
favourable end, glass ionomer in the middle, and eugenol-based and
corrosion-prone materials toward the less favourable end. The

8.8 Soft-tissue and aesthetic considerations
Two practical caveats deserve mention. First, some bioceramics can
discolour the tooth and the overlying gingiva through their bismuth- or
iron-oxide radiopacifiers; near the visible gingival margin, white or
heavy-metal-free formulations are preferred. Second, where a material
or restoration margin lies close to the gingiva, respect for the biologic
width — the soft-tissue attachment coronal to the bone — remains
essential; even the most biocompatible material will not compensate for
a margin that violates the attachment and provokes chronic
inflammation.

8.9 The peri-implant interface
The reaction of tissues to materials is not confined to natural teeth. A
dental implant sits in the same periodontal environment, and its success
depends on two interfaces: the bone–implant interface, where
osseointegration binds living bone directly to the surface (discussed in
Chapter Five), and the soft-tissue interface, where the gingiva forms a
seal around the abutment. Bioceramics contribute at both. Bioactive
coatings such as hydroxyapatite encourage bone to grow onto the
implant, while ceramic (zirconia) abutment surfaces are associated with
a favourable soft-tissue response and low plaque affinity, helping to
maintain the peri-implant seal. The same principle applies as around a
tooth: a stable, well-adapted, biocompatible surface at the tissue margin
protects the supporting structures, whereas roughness, contamination or
a compromised seal invites inflammation — peri-implant mucositis and, if
it progresses, peri-implantitis. Calcium-phosphate and bioactive-glass
grafts further support the bone that must surround and stabilise the
implant.

8.10 Endodontic sealers and the periodontal
response
Nowhere is the difference between material families clearer than in the
behaviour of root-canal sealers, which are extruded or diffuse to the
apical and lateral periodontium and remain there in intimate contact with
the tissues. The biocompatibility of a sealer is therefore a determinant of
periradicular healing, and it has been studied by comparing the
long-established epoxy-resin sealers (typified by AH Plus) with the newer
calcium-silicate-based sealers on two fronts: the inflammatory response
they provoke and the osteogenic (bone- and cementum-forming)
potential they support (Dassieu, 2023).
On the first front, the literature consistently reports a lower inflammatory
response to calcium-silicate sealers than to epoxy-resin sealers, whose
freshly set surfaces tend to provoke a stronger initial reaction;
cytotoxicity generally falls as calcium-silicate materials set and mature
(Zhou et al., 2015; Saygili et al., 2017; Kapralos et al., 2022; Özdemir
and Kopac, 2022). Subcutaneous-implantation and histological studies
show the same direction of effect in vivo (Kohsar et al., 2022).
On the second front, calcium-silicate sealers show a positive osteogenic
potential — up-regulating mineralisation markers and encouraging
hard-tissue formation — where epoxy-resin sealers tend to be neutral or
negative. Comparative laboratory studies on periodontal-ligament and
bone-forming cells report greater expression of osteogenic markers and
mineralisation with calcium-silicate sealers (Giacomino et al., 2019; Lee
et al., 2019; Alves Silva et al., 2020), consistent with systematic and
narrative reviews of the class (Donnermeyer et al., 2019; Lim et al.,
2020). The mechanism is the one described earlier in this chapter:
calcium release, alkalinity and apatite formation create a surface that
periodontal cells tolerate and build upon.

What this means clinically
• Where a sealer may reach the periodontium, calcium-silicate materials
tend to provoke less inflammation and better support hard-tissue healing
than epoxy-resin sealers.
• The evidence is largely laboratory and animal-based; long-term clinical
comparisons are still limited.
8.11 Evidence and gaps
The evidence that bioceramics favour periodontal healing is strong at the
histological and animal level and is supported by clinical healing of
periradicular and periodontal defects. As elsewhere in this field, the
principal gap is long-term, randomised clinical data comparing materials
on periodontal outcomes over many years. The consistency of the
biological signal, however, gives clinicians good reason to prefer
bioceramics wherever a material will meet the periodontium.
Chapter summary
• Many endodontic procedures place a material permanently against the
periodontium; the tissue response drives the outcome.
• Traditional materials are tolerated at best; eugenol- and corrosion-based
ones can be irritant.
• Bioceramics favour cementogenesis, ligament reattachment and bone
healing with little inflammation.
• The mechanism is the familiar one: high pH, calcium release and apatite
formation guiding periodontal cells.
• Prefer white / heavy-metal-free materials near the gingival margin, and
respect the biologic width.

Conclusion
Introduced clinically in the 1990s as a root-end filling, mineral trioxide
aggregate opened a field. Its uses have broadened continuously, and a
growing family of bioceramic and hybrid materials has followed it into
practice. These materials share a common calcium-silicate chemistry and
a common signature — high alkalinity, calcium release and apatite
formation — but differ in the mechanical properties that flow from their
composition, above all compressive strength and setting time.
What unites them is biocompatibility and outstanding physicochemical
behaviour, and a demonstrated capacity to overcome limitations of
earlier endodontic materials. In doing so they have widened the dentist's
reach: teeth with open apices, candidates for pulp capping and
pulpotomy, retrograde fillings after apicoectomy, and the repair of
perforations and resorptions can now be treated more predictably than
before.
This expanded edition has set that endodontic story within a wider
frame. Bioceramics belong to a family of dental materials that reaches
from the all-ceramic crown to the bone graft, and their behaviour is best
understood alongside the two disciplines that meet them most closely:
adhesion, which provides the coronal seal that protects the bioceramic
work beneath it and increasingly shares its bioactive ambitions; and the
response of the periodontium, which is where the regenerative promise
of bioceramics is most visibly realised, in new cementum, reattached
ligament and healing bone. Seen together, these threads describe a shift
in dentistry from materials that merely fill space to materials that seal,
bond and help tissue to rebuild.
The evidence gap
• In-vitro and animal evidence for bioceramics is strong and consistent.
• Long-term, randomised, double-blind clinical trials remain scarce for
most indications.
• Robust, well-designed studies with adequate follow-up are the priority
for the field.

-- 81 of 132 --

Bioceramic in Dentistry 82
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
The promise of these in-vitro results now needs confirmation from
long-term randomised, double-blind controlled trials over clinically
meaningful periods. For most indications the high-quality clinical
literature remains sparse. Closing that gap — with well-designed studies
and adequate follow-up — is the task that will determine how far
bioceramics reshape endodontic practice in the years ahead.

Clinical protocols at a glance

Expanded & Illustrated Edition
Root-end (retrograde) filling
1. Reflect a full-thickness flap and gain access to the periapical lesion
under magnification.
2. Resect the apical 3 mm of root and curette the lesion.
3. Prepare a retrograde cavity along the canal axis with ultrasonic tips.
4. Dry the cavity; place and condense a bioceramic cement (e.g. MTA)
as the root-end fill.
5. Confirm the seal, irrigate, reposition the flap and suture; review
radiographically.
Note
MTA gives excellent sealing and periradicular regeneration; fast-set repair
materials ease handling in a moist field.

Bioceramic in Dentistry 98
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
Furcal / lateral perforation repair
1. Achieve haemostasis and isolate the perforation; control moisture.
2. If needed, place an internal matrix (e.g. resorbable collagen) to
prevent extrusion.
3. Place a bioceramic cement (MTA or Biodentine) against the defect
with light condensation.
4. Verify the seal; where slow-setting cement is used, protect with a
moist pellet until set.
5. Restore the remaining access and provide a definitive coronal seal.
Note
Prefer white or heavy-metal-free materials near the gingival margin to avoid
discolouration.

Bioceramic in Dentistry 99
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
Apical plug (apexification) in an open-apex tooth
1. Disinfect the canal by gentle chemo-mechanical debridement;
inter-visit calcium hydroxide if indicated.
2. Dry the canal and place an apical plug of bioceramic cement
(~4 mm), condensed to the apex.
3. Confirm plug position and adaptation radiographically.
4. Backfill the remaining canal and place a bonded coronal restoration
to reinforce the root.
Note
MTA and comparable cements give earlier filling and better fracture
resistance than long-term calcium hydroxide.

Bioceramic in Dentistry 100
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
Regenerative endodontic procedure (immature necrotic
tooth)
1. Disinfect with copious irrigation and an intracanal medicament (e.g.
triple antibiotic paste).
2. At the second visit, induce apical bleeding to form a blood clot, or
place platelet-rich plasma as a scaffold.
3. Place a bioceramic cement (white MTA) as a coronal plug over the
scaffold.
4. Seal with a definitive restoration; monitor for continued root
development.
Note
Aims to regenerate — not merely replace — the pulp-dentine complex;
outcomes require long-term follow-up.

Bioceramic in Dentistry 101
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
Direct pulp capping / partial pulpotomy
1. Confirm a favourable diagnosis and achieve haemostasis at the
exposure.
2. For partial pulpotomy, remove superficial unhealthy pulp to sound
tissue.
3. Place a calcium-silicate material (MTA or Biodentine) directly over the
pulp wound.
4. Seal with a well-adapted restoration to prevent bacterial leakage;
review vitality.
Note
Calcium-silicate materials outperform calcium hydroxide for dentine-bridge
quality and clinical success.

Bioceramic in Dentistry 102
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
Adhesive restoration over a calcium-silicate base
1. Place and allow the calcium-silicate material (e.g. Biodentine) to set
and begin to mature.
2. Isolate the tooth (rubber dam) and clean the surface, avoiding
eugenol-containing materials beforehand.
3. Apply the chosen adhesive (a universal or etch-and-rinse system) per
the manufacturer's instructions.
4. Build up with composite in increments, curing each thoroughly.
5. Check the margin and occlusion; a sound seal protects the
bioceramic beneath.
Note
Where possible, delay definitive bonding until the calcium-silicate surface
has matured to improve adhesion.

Bioceramic in Dentistry 103
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
Bonded fibre post in an endodontically treated tooth
1. Confirm an adequate apical seal and remove gutta-percha/sealer to
the planned post depth, leaving 4–5 mm apically.
2. Prefer a resin-compatible (non-eugenol) sealer where a bonded post
is anticipated.
3. Isolate, condition the root canal and apply the adhesive; control
moisture carefully in the deep canal.
4. Cement the fibre post with a (often dual-cure) resin cement and build
the core.
5. Restore with a crown that provides a ferrule and a sound coronal
seal.
Note
Bonding into the canal is technique-sensitive; isolation and eugenol-free
residues are decisive.

Bioceramic in Dentistry 104
Expanded & Illustrated Edition
Perforation repair protecting the periodontium
1. Locate the perforation and control haemorrhage and moisture.
2. If the defect is large or faces the periodontium, place an internal
matrix to prevent extrusion.
3. Seal with a calcium-silicate bioceramic (MTA or Biodentine) adapted
to sound margins.
4. Prefer white / heavy-metal-free material near the gingival margin to
avoid discolouration.
5. Confirm the seal, then restore; respect the biologic width at the
tissue margin.
Note
A biocompatible seal favours cementum and bone repair; it cannot
compensate for a margin that invades the periodontal attachment.

About this edition
Bioceramic in Dentistry — Expanded & Illustrated Edition
This edition preserves the text, scholarship and full bibliography of the
original work by Dr. Saad Alsaif and Dr. Khalid Al Otaibi (Prince
Abdulrahman Advanced Dental Institute, Riyadh, 2026). The endodontic
chapters and their citations are the authors’ own work.
Added for this edition are twenty-one original schematic illustrations and
comparative charts, section summaries and clinical call-outs, two
background chapters placing endodontic bioceramics in the wider
context of dentistry and materials science, and a set of quick-reference
appendices. The illustrations are diagrammatic, not clinical images; the
chart values are representative figures drawn from the cited literature;
and the background chapters are written as established materials
science rather than as new, separately-cited claims.
